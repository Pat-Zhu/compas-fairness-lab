Activity 1 · 3 minutes

Who would you rather trust with your liberty?

If you were a defendant awaiting trial, would you rather be assessed by COMPAS or by a human judge?

Discuss: What would you need to know before you could answer confidently?
Facilitator notes

Collect: comparative error rates, transparency, data used, appeal rights, stakes, and human oversight. Do not resolve the question yet; you will ask it again at the end.

Activity 2 · 5 minutes

Two fairness claims. Both can be mathematically correct.

ProPublica lens

Errors should not fall differently by race.

False-positive rate · Black defendants44.9%
False-positive rate · White defendants23.5%

Concern: Black defendants who did not later reoffend were more likely to be labeled high risk.

Northpointe lens

The same score should mean roughly the same risk.

Example: score 7 · White≈60%
Example: score 7 · Black≈61%

Defense: the score was approximately calibrated across groups.

If both sides can be mathematically correct, what are they actually disagreeing about?

They are disagreeing about the normative meaning of fairness, not simply the arithmetic. One prioritizes error-rate parity; the other prioritizes calibration.
Facilitator notes

This directly covers: What did ProPublica say was unfair? What did Northpointe say was fair? If both are mathematically correct, what are they actually disagreeing about?

Activity 3 · 7–8 minutes

Pick a fairness principle — and a cost you are willing to accept.

Split into four groups. Each group gets a different role. Choose what you would prioritize, then name the unfairness or risk you are willing to tolerate in exchange.

A

Defendants / civil-rights advocates

Priority: avoid wrongly labeling low-risk people as dangerous. Which mistake matters most?

B

Model developers / Northpointe

Priority: keep scores meaningful and calibrated across groups. What breaks if calibration is sacrificed?

C

Judges / public-safety officials

Priority: avoid releasing people classified as low risk who later reoffend. How should false negatives matter?

D

Community / ethicists

Priority: challenge the premise. Should predicted future risk determine liberty at all?

Group deliverable: “We prioritize ___ because ___. We are willing to tolerate more ___ because ___.”
Facilitator notes

Give groups 4–5 minutes and 30 seconds each to report out. Push each group to identify who bears the cost of its preferred definition of fairness.

Activity 4 · 4 minutes

“We don’t use race.” Is that enough?

If removing race as an input does not remove racial disparities, what does that tell us about the claim “we don’t use race”?

Historical institutions→Policing / opportunity patterns→ Observed data→Correlated features→Risk score→Decision

Race can be absent as an explicit column while race-related structure remains in labels, proxies, and the data-generating process.

Facilitator notes

Ask for one example of a proxy variable or a label shaped by institutions. Avoid implying that any correlated feature is automatically illegitimate.

Activity 5 · 5 minutes

Threshold Lab

You are the policymaker. Move the threshold and watch one kind of error fall while another rises.

—True positives
—False positives
—True negatives
—False negatives
Classified high risk / detained

Illustrative toy dataset for classroom use — not actual COMPAS data.

Discuss

1. Which threshold would you choose if “high risk” could mean pretrial detention?

2. Would you choose the same threshold if “high risk” only triggered voluntary counseling?

3. What does this tell us about whether fairness can be separated from stakes?

Facilitator notes

This tees up the questions about pretrial liberty and the moral weight of false positives versus false negatives.

99.9%

Imagine COMPAS is now almost perfect.

Equal false-positive rates. Equal false-negative rates. Calibrated scores. 99.9% accurate.

Would you now be comfortable using it to help decide who sits in jail before trial?

Activity 7 · 4 minutes

Who gets to define fairness?

You have exactly 10 influence tokens. Allocate them across the people who should have the most say in choosing the fairness definition used to evaluate a system like COMPAS.

Tokens remaining: 10

Discuss: Was choosing calibration versus equal error rates really an engineering decision, or a governance decision disguised as one?
